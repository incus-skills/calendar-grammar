- **Core Subjects:** Focus deeply on **Mathematics, English, Science, and Latin** (or other classical languages), as these are central to the **Election** exam for 13+ entry and the **Founders’ Scholarship** exams for 16+ entry.

You're right, and it's a well-known problem. The state curriculum is breadth-oriented and assessment-standardised, while Winchester's process is depth-oriented and idiosyncratic. Here are the specific gaps and how to close them:

## What the state curriculum simply doesn't teach

- **Verbal and non-verbal reasoning** — not in the National Curriculum at all. The ISEB Pre-Test (Year 6) and the 16+ aptitude test both rely heavily on it. You have to learn the question types (analogy, classification, spatial rotation, letter codes) from scratch.
- **Latin and Greek** — almost no state secondary offers them before Year 9, and very few before Year 12. Yet they're optional Election papers at 13+ and a strong differentiator.
- **The "General Paper"** — a Winchester-specific assessment that tests cross-disciplinary reasoning, logic, and broad knowledge with no single syllabus. There's no state equivalent.
- **The Founder's essay paper** — questions like _"Is there really any free will?"_ or _"Are there any books you would ban?"_ are deliberately content-agnostic. They test how you think, not what you've memorised. No GCSE or A-Level prep covers this.
- **Exam technique for inference-heavy English** — Winchester's English papers are longer than typical 11+/13+ papers, with heavy dialogue, advanced vocabulary, and marks awarded for commentary on writer's technique rather than plot summary.

## What the state curriculum teaches but at a different depth/pace

- **Maths** — the state KS3 maths syllabus is the floor, but Winchester's papers go into more complex problem-solving. The Election Maths 2 paper is significantly above standard CE level.
- **Science** — state KS3 science is broad but shallow. Winchester's paper splits into Biology, Chemistry, and Physics with more application-style questions.
- **History and Geography** — taught at state, but the CE-level depth (e.g., tectonic processes, specific historical causation) often isn't reached until Year 9 or 10 in state schools.

## Practical curriculum to fill the gaps

**From age 10–11 (Year 5/6):**

- Weekly verbal and non-verbal reasoning practice (CGP, Bond, or ISEB-specific resources)
- Expand reading well beyond the school list — non-fiction (history, science, philosophy-lite), classic fiction, and the _Guardian_ or _New Statesman_
- Start Latin if at all possible (even a few hours a week with a tutor or online course)
- Begin timed comprehension practice with inference-heavy passages

**From age 12–13 (Year 7/8):**

- Targeted tuition in Maths (aim for 11+/13+ scholarship level, not just GCSE)
- Practice the General Paper format — read broadly, write short analytical pieces on cross-disciplinary topics
- Start structured essay writing on open questions (the Founder's style) — not GCSE-style "discuss with reference to the text" but genuine argumentative writing
- If you can, add a second language (French/Greek) beyond what the state school offers

**From age 15–16 (Year 10/11, for 16+ entry):**

- The 16+ aptitude test (vocabulary, maths, non-verbal reasoning) is the gatekeeper — practice all three
- Subject-specific interview prep for your three A-Level choices: the interview tests _how you think about unseen material_, not whether you know the answer
- The Founder's essay paper — practise writing 30–40 minute essays on philosophical/ethical questions with no preparation. Read sample questions on Winchester's site and write responses, then get them critiqued.
- Build a CV of independent intellectual activity — self-directed reading, a research project, a podcast, a blog. The interview will ask _"Describe a self-directed learning project you've undertaken"_ and _"How do you stay intellectually curious beyond your school curriculum?"_

## The meta-skill

The single biggest gap isn't a subject — it's **the habit of thinking out loud about unfamiliar problems**. State education rewards correct answers to known questions. Winchester rewards the visible process of reasoning through something you've never seen before. Practising timed, open-ended writing and articulating your thought process (even when you don't know the answer) is the highest-leverage thing you can do.
