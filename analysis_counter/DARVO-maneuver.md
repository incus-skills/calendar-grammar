# DARVO maneuver (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender)"

**DARVO** is an acronym for **Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender**. Originally coined by psychologist Jennifer Freyd to describe the behavior of perpetrators of sexual abuse, it has become a standard term in political discourse to identify a specific manipulation strategy used to evade accountability.

### The Three-Step Mechanism

In political contexts, DARVO functions as a "three-beat play" that flips the narrative when a figure or party is confronted with wrongdoing:

1.  **Deny:** The actor issues an immediate, categorical denial of the event or allegation, often before facts are fully known. Common phrases include labeling the issue a "hoax," "witch hunt," or "nothingburger."
2.  **Attack:** Instead of addressing the evidence, the actor attacks the credibility, motives, or character of the accuser (e.g., journalists, opposition parties, or whistleblowers). This shifts the focus from the original allegation to the perceived unfairness of the inquiry.
3.  **Reverse Victim and Offender:** The actor claims that _they_ are the true victims of persecution, harassment, or political bias. This reframes the original accuser as the aggressor and the accused as the aggrieved party, often mobilizing their base around this new grievance.

### Impact on Political Discourse

The primary function of DARVO in politics is to **confuse bystanders** and **punish oversight**. By inverting the roles of victim and offender, it creates an "asymmetric burden" where the accuser must defend both their original claim and their own character.

- **Erosion of Trust:** It undermines trust in institutions and the media by framing legitimate scrutiny as "abuse" or "oppression."
- **Mobilization:** The "victim" narrative is highly effective for fundraising and rallying in-group loyalty, turning a scandal into a tool for political consolidation.
- **Chilling Effect:** It discourages future whistleblowing or criticism, as potential accusers anticipate being smeared rather than heard.
