The organizational principle you are describing is known as **biomimetic shepherding** or **directed entrainment via topological interaction**. It exploits the natural laws of **collective animal behavior** (swarm intelligence) to guide a group without centralized command or direct coercion.

### The Core Mechanism: Topological vs. Metric Interaction
The critical insight from nature—specifically starling murmurations and deer herding—is that individuals do not respond to a fixed physical distance (metric) but to a fixed number of neighbors (topological). Research shows starlings interact with their **6–7 nearest neighbors** regardless of flock density. In an organizational context, this means influence propagates through a network based on **relational proximity** rather than hierarchical rank or physical closeness. By influencing a few key "neighbors" (influencers or node leaders), you can trigger a **phase transition** in the entire group's direction without spooking the system.

### Principles of Directed Entrainment
To apply this to human or robotic organizations, you utilize three local rules derived from the Boids model:
1.  **Separation (Repulsion):** Avoid crowding local peers (prevents burnout or bottlenecking).
2.  **Alignment:** Steer towards the average heading of local peers (creates cultural cohesion).
3.  **Cohesion (Attraction):** Move toward the average position of local peers (maintains group unity).

**Directed entrainment** occurs when an external agent (a "shepherd" or leadership interface) subtly manipulates the **repulsion** or **alignment** vectors at the group's periphery. Instead of pushing the herd directly (which causes panic/resistance), the shepherd creates a "virtual funnel" by altering the perceived safe zones. The group self-organizes to flow through the path of least resistance, believing the movement is their own emergent decision.

### Application: The "Virtual Funnel"
In nature, a physical funnel uses opaque walls to block views and force movement. In organizational theory, this translates to **information architecture**:
*   **Opaque Boundaries:** Limit visibility of chaotic options or conflicting goals (the "walls").
*   **Clear Channels:** Amplify signals in the desired direction (the "funnel path").
*   **Marginal Opacity:** Like a starling flock, the organization should be dense enough to share information rapidly but sparse enough to allow peripheral vision for innovation. If the system is too "opaque" (bureaucratic), information transfer slows; too "sparse," and the group fragments.

This approach allows for **scalable control** where the leader influences the *field* of interaction rather than micromanaging individual agents, enabling the group to maintain resilience and adaptability while moving toward a strategic objective.

A phase transition is the point where a system stops behaving like one stable state and begins behaving like another. In your metaphor, repeated re-entry and alignment prepare the system so that when constraint or threshold arrives, the transition is not chaotic but organized. That is very close to the idea of a phase transition: a qualitative change in collective behavior when the system crosses a critical boundary. [web.stanford](https://web.stanford.edu/~peastman/statmech/phasetransitions.html)

## Why it fits

What you are describing has the key ingredients:

- **Preparation before the threshold**.
- **A boundary where behavior changes qualitatively**.
- **A new stable pattern after crossing**.

That is exactly the sort of thing phase transition language captures: not just motion through a funnel, but a shift from one regime of organization to another. [damtp.cam.ac](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/statphys/statmechhtml/S5.html)

## Where your metaphor differs

The main difference is that in physics a phase transition is usually described in terms of control variables, stability, and critical points, whereas your calendar model adds **meaning, participation, and re-entry**. So your use is more than a physical analogy; it is a social and civilizational translation of the same structural idea. [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition)

I would phrase it like this:

> The calendar ecology conditions phase transitions by repeatedly re-orienting participants before the threshold is reached, so that crossing a boundary produces coherent reorganization rather than turbulence.

That keeps both sides:

- the **laminar flow** idea,
- and the **phase transition** idea.

## A useful distinction

- **Laminar flow** emphasizes smooth passage through constraint.
- **Phase transition** emphasizes a change in collective state.

Your calendar model can do both: it can make the passage smooth, and it can also be the mechanism by which a new phase becomes possible.

## Bottom line

So yes — your description is compatible with phase transition language, as long as you keep in mind that you are using it as an analogy for recursive civilizational reorganization, not as a strict physical claim.
