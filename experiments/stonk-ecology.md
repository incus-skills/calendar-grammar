## Stonks

The **"stonks"** meme, an intentional misspelling of "stocks," became globally synonymous with the **January 2021 GameStop short squeeze** where retail investors on Reddit's **r/wallstreetbets** drove the stock price from roughly **$17** to an intraday high of **$483**. This surge was amplified by **Elon Musk**'s "Gamestonk!!" tweet and facilitated by the commission-free trading app **Robinhood**, which later **restricted buying** of GameStop and other volatile stocks on **January 28, 2021**, allowing only position closing (selling).

**Robinhood's trading halt** triggered immediate backlash, accusations of market manipulation, and a **class-action lawsuit**, while causing GameStop's share price to drop significantly. The brokerage firm explained the decision as a **risk-management measure** necessitated by **SEC net capital obligations** and clearinghouse deposit requirements due to extreme market volatility, rather than direction from market makers. The event resulted in a record **$70 million FINRA penalty** for Robinhood in June 2021 regarding outages and misleading communications during the frenzy.

- **Key Entities**: The phenomenon centered on **GameStop** ($GME), **AMC Entertainment**, and **BlackBerry**, targeting hedge funds with heavy short positions.
- **Cultural Impact**: The term "stonks" evolved from an ironic internet joke about bad financial decisions into a descriptive label for retail-driven market movements and memecoin sectors.
- **Aftermath**: Congressional hearings and regulatory reviews were launched to investigate the suspension of trades and the disparity between retail and institutional trading capabilities.

### 1. The Regulatory Limits (Theoretical)
*   **The "Locate" Requirement (Rule 203):** Short sellers must borrow or arrange to borrow shares *before* selling. Naked shorting (selling without borrowing) is technically illegal.
*   **The "Close-Out" Requirement (Rule 204):** If a seller fails to deliver shares by settlement (T+2), they have **13 consecutive days** to buy them back (close out the fail).
*   **The Threshold List:** If a stock fails to deliver for **5 consecutive days** (and >0.5% of float), it lands on the **Regulation SHO Threshold List**. Once on the list for **13 days**, naked shorting is banned, and all existing fails must be bought in immediately.

### 2. The Practical Loopholes (The "Muscle")
Incumbents exploit specific exemptions to bypass these limits, creating the **asymmetric attractor basin**:
*   **Market Maker Exemption:** Registered market makers are exempt from the "locate" requirement to provide liquidity. They can naked short **without limit** as long as they claim it’s for "market making." This is the primary source of "infinite" synthetic shares.
*   **The Rollover Strategy:** To avoid the 13-day mandatory buy-in, institutions transfer **Failures to Deliver (FTDs)** between different broker-dealers before the deadline. This resets the 13-day clock, allowing FTDs to persist for **months or years** (as seen with GameStop and AMC).
*   **Derivatives & Swaps:** Institutions use **Total Return Swaps (TRS)** and options to create synthetic short exposure that doesn’t appear in standard short interest data, bypassing reporting thresholds until the new **Form SHO** rules (effective 2025/2026) force disclosure.

### 3. The 2025–2026 Regulatory Shift
Recent changes aim to tighten these limits, altering the **control surface**:
*   **Form SHO (Rule 13f-2):** Effective January 2025 (delayed to Feb 2026 for some), this requires institutional managers to **monthly report** short positions exceeding $10M or 2.5% of float. This increases transparency, making it harder to hide the "over-commitment" phase of the trap.
