Whether "cybernetic hypnosis"—or more broadly, digital/algorithmic persuasion techniques often discussed under terms like cyber-hypnosis—constitutes a form of **rhetoric** depends on how you define rhetoric.

In communication theory, philosophy, and media studies, rhetoric is fundamentally the art of persuasion, using symbols, arguments, structures, and cues to influence an audience's beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors.

Evaluating whether digital persuasion mechanisms align with rhetorical practice involves looking at several key perspectives:

---

### 1. Where They Overlap (Why it can be considered rhetoric)

- **Intent to Influence:** Traditional rhetoric aims to move an audience toward a specific viewpoint or action. Digital interfaces, user experience (UX) design, and algorithmic feeds are purposefully engineered to guide human attention, shape choices, and elicit specific behaviors (such as clicking, buying, or staying engaged).
- **Non-Verbal and Visual Arguments:** Rhetoric extends far beyond spoken or written words. Visual design, pacing, architecture, and interactive cues function as modern persuasive symbols—similar to how classical rhetoric used visual pageantry, tone, and delivery.
- **Pacing and Leading:** Just as a classical orator builds a narrative arc to guide an audience's emotional state, digital systems use continuous feedback loops (notifications, infinite scrolls, micro-rewards) to pace and lead a user's psychological state.

### 2. Where They Differ (Why some argue it transcends traditional rhetoric)

- **Bypassing Deliberation:** Classical rhetoric relies heavily on symbols, arguments, and appeals (logos, ethos, pathos) that—ideally—interact with a conscious, reasoning mind. In contrast, digital stimuli designed to induce a trance-like or reactive state often target cognitive shortcuts, sensory overload, and automatic reflexes rather than active persuasion.
- **Algorithmic Personalization:** Traditional rhetoric is typically one-to-many (an orator speaking to a crowd). Digital persuasion is hyper-individualized, using real-time feedback loops and behavioral data to adapt its "arguments" to a specific user's psychological vulnerabilities.
- **Automation:** Rhetoric is traditionally an expressive human act of communication. Algorithmic environments automate the process of influence continuously, without a human author actively constructing an argument in real time.

---

### Summary

If **rhetoric** is broadly defined as _any systematic technology or art designed to shape human perception and behavior_, then digital and cybernetic influence techniques can certainly be seen as an automated, technologically mediated evolution of rhetoric. However, because they often operate on subconscious loops, sensory overload, and behavioral reflex rather than symbolic argumentation, many theorists view them as a shift **beyond traditional rhetoric** and into the realm of behavioral engineering or cybernetic governance.
