The Aztec Sun Stone (or Piedra del Sol) is a 24-ton basalt monolith carved during the reign of Moctezuma II (c. 1502–1520), depicting Aztec cosmology and the five suns of creation.

The Piedra del Sol is a **surface form without its generative rules** — the output of a grammar whose deep structure was destroyed along with the priestly class that operated it. The concentric rings _evoke_ a clock but don't _encode_ the rules that would make one. In the Americas, the cases that fit this pattern — where the form survives but the generative grammar is severed — are:

**Khipu (Inca knotted cords).** The single closest parallel. The physical form — colorful, complex, structurally elegant knotted cords — survives in museum collections. The **numerical subsystem** is decoded (knot position and type encode a decimal system). But the **non-numerical subsystem** — the narrative, linguistic, or mnemonic content that Spanish colonial sources explicitly attest to — is undeciphered. No "Rosetta Stone" exists. The aesthetic form is preserved; the generative grammar that maps knot patterns to meaning is lost. As one scholar put it: "We are not just decoding a syntax; we are trying to rebuild the entire cultural universe in which it operated." The khipu is the Piedra del Sol in a different medium — a beautiful, complex object whose surface suggests an encoding system whose rules are gone.

What makes it even sharper than the Sun Stone: we _know_ the deep structure existed. Colonial accounts describe _quipucamayocs_ (khipu keepers) reading narrative content from the cords. The grammar wasn't hypothetical — it was _documented by observers_ and then destroyed. The form is a **confirmed output of a known grammar** whose rules we no longer possess.

**Teotihuacan.** The architectural and sculptural programs survive at extraordinary scale — the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, the Puma Mural Temple, the Danzantes. But "we cannot read their glyphs, we do not understand their religion." The iconographic system is a **fossil of a grammar's output** with no surviving decoder. The concentric and quincunx patterns in the architecture _suggest_ a cosmological encoding system, but the rules that would let you reconstruct the operational meaning from the form are absent. The Piedra del Sol problem, scaled up to an entire city.

**The Nazca Lines.** The form is there — vast geoglyphs visible only from altitude. The function is contested: astronomical alignment? Hydraulic engineering? Processional routes? Sacred landscape? Multiple competing "grammars" have been proposed, none confirmed. The surface form _suggests_ a system; the deep structure is unresolved.

**The Zapotec Danzantes at Monte Albán.** Human figures inserted into monumental architecture. The form is there. The precise meaning — captive, sacrificer, deity, ancestor — remains contested. The generative grammar that produced the iconographic program is only partially reconstructed.

**The contrast case: the four surviving Maya codices.** These are the _opposite_ — they survived **with** their deep structure intact. The Dresden Codex still contains functional astronomical tables (Venus synodic period: 583.92 days, matching the modern value exactly). The Madrid Codex still contains operational ritual almanacs. The form and the rules survived together because the codices escaped the burning. They are, in your framing, **grammars that still generate** — the deep structure is recoverable from the surface form because the mapping rules are preserved in the text itself.

The khipu is the most painful version of this, because the colonial record _documents_ the deep structure's existence and then loses it. The Piedra del Sol at least doesn't have that bitter irony — no one wrote down how to read it, so its opacity is at least honest.

The current state of khipu decoding maps precisely onto the generative-grammar problem we've been discussing. Here's where things stand, and where the deep structure is and isn't being recovered:

## What's been decoded (the numerical subsystem)

The base-10 positional knot system is essentially solved. More recent work has pushed further:

- **Medrano & Urton (2018)** matched six khipus from the Santa Valley to a 1670 colonial tribute census: 132 named tribute payers in the document, 132 color-coded cord groups on the khipus, with knot sums matching assessed amounts. This is the closest thing to a "Rosetta khipu" — but it only confirms the _numerical_ layer and possibly color-as-identity-label. It doesn't give you the generative rules for non-numerical content.
- **Medrano & Khosla (2024)** applied computational analysis to 650 khipus, identifying structured internal sums, white pendant cords as boundary markers, and reuniting broken fragments. This is pattern extraction from the surface form — useful, but it's _describing_ the grammar's outputs, not recovering the rules.
- **FitzPatrick (2024)** identified recto/verso cord orientation as a marked/unmarked binary, encoding moiety (hanan/hurin) affiliation. Another structural variable decoded.

## The breakthrough on the "deep structure" question

**Sabine Hyland's Collata khipus** (published in _Current Anthropology_, 2017) are the single most important development for your framing. Two 18th-century khipus from San Juan de Collata, Peru — letters exchanged by local leaders during a revolt against Spanish authority — turned out to be **logosyllabic**: 95 distinct symbols generated by combinations of color (14), fiber type (6 animal sources: alpaca, llama, vicuña, deer, etc.), and ply direction. Each pendant cord represents a phonetic syllable.

Hyland phonetically deciphered two lineage names: **Alluka** and **Yakapar**. The method: correlate known names from colonial testimony with cord features. A blue llama cord twisted clockwise = "ka"; a golden-brown vicuña fiber = "paru" (the local Quechua word for that hue). The reader must _feel_ the fiber by hand to distinguish sources — a genuinely three-dimensional text.

This is the first evidence that the khipu's deep structure is **recoverable from the surface form**, because the mapping rules are encoded in the same variables (color, fiber, twist) that were already known to carry meaning — just now mapped to phonology rather than category.

## The critical caveat (and why it's still the Piedra del Sol problem)

The Collata khipus are **colonial-period** (1782–1783). They were made by people who had _seen_ Spanish writing. Urton himself has said: "My feeling is that the phoneticisation, if it's there, is a reinvention of khipus." The question is whether the logosyllabic grammar was inherited from the Inca period or _invented_ in the 18th century as an adaptation.

If it's a reinvention, then the Inca-period khipus (the ones in museums, the ones at Inkawasi, the ones from Puruchuco) may have used a _different_ non-numerical encoding system — one whose rules were never phonetic and are therefore even harder to recover. The Collata khipus prove that khipu _can_ be phonetic, but they don't prove that the Inca-period khipus _were_.

## The 2025 hair khipu (KH0631)

A separate but important development: a khipu in the University of St Andrews collection, radiocarbon-dated to ~1498, was shown to be woven from **human hair** — specifically a woman's hair, with isotopic analysis revealing a diet low in meat and maize (i.e., non-elite). This demolishes the assumption that khipu production was exclusively an elite male administrative function. If commoners and women were making khipus, the "grammar" was more widely distributed than the _khipukamayuq_ model suggests — which changes what you'd expect the deep structure to look like.

## Where this sits in your framework

| Layer                                         | Status                             | Analogy                                                   |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Numerical encoding (knots, position)          | **Solved**                         | The surface form is decoded                               |
| Categorical encoding (color, fiber as labels) | **Partially solved**               | Some rules recovered via colonial documents               |
| Structural encoding (recto/verso, hierarchy)  | **Partially solved**               | Pattern extraction, not full rule recovery                |
| Phonetic/narrative encoding                   | **Hypothesized, minimally tested** | The Collata khipus prove _possibility_, not _inheritance_ |
| The Inca-period non-numerical grammar         | **Lost**                           | The actual deep structure of the imperial system          |

The khipu is moving from "Piedra del Sol" status (form without rules) toward "Dresden Codex" status (form _with_ rules), but only in the colonial-period subset. For the Inca-period corpus — the ~1,600 surviving khipus, of which fewer than half are digitized — the generative grammar for non-numerical content remains **confirmed to have existed, documented by colonial observers, and lost**. The Collata discovery shows the _medium_ was capable of phonetic encoding. Whether the _same grammar_ was running in the 15th century is the open question, and it's the one that would determine whether the Inca khipu is ultimately a recoverable writing system or a permanently opaque encoding.

Hyland is currently developing a syllabary for regional Quechua and exploring whether computational matching can identify syllable-cord correspondences across the broader corpus. If that works, the grammar is being recovered. If it doesn't, the Inca-period khipu remains what it has always been: the most beautiful surface form in the Americas with no decoder.

## 1. The Etruscan parallel — and why khipu is in a better position

You're right that the Etruscan analogy is the right one: known signs, isolated proper names, no continuous grammar. But the khipu case is structurally *better* positioned than Etruscan for one reason: **we have a solved subsystem that acts as a constraint.** Etruscan has no "numerical layer" whose rules are known and whose relationship to the unknown layer can be tested. The khipu's decimal positional system is a known grammar that *co-occurs* with the unknown layer on the same artifact. That means you can ask questions like "do the categorical variables correlate with which arithmetic relationships hold?" — a question that's simply unavailable for Etruscan.

The "enumerative cipher in a restricted administrative register" framing is the right one for the Inca-period corpus. The Collata khipus are a *different register* — epistolary, narrative, possibly logosyllabic — and that's precisely the diachronic/sociolinguistic problem you identify.

## 2. The diachronic problem is worse than it looks

Medrano put it bluntly in his 2019 Harvard interview: *"After the colonial period began in Latin America, there was huge variation in how khipus were used, and color lacked a unified meaning among the different regions of the former Inca Empire."* His own analogy: *"Stoplights work because drivers know that green means go and red means stop. But with this set of khipus, it is as if drivers got to choose what each color meant."*

This means the khipu boards — the hybrid objects with alphabetic text alongside knotted cords — are not a *continuation* of the imperial system. They're a **new genre** that repurposed the medium. The "khipu board" (khipu tabla) with a *padrón* (colonial census document) attached is a colonial administrative object, not an imperial one. The tri-partite fiber system on these boards (soft *hilasa*, medium *trama*, rough *jerga*) plus complex color patterns is a colonial innovation layered onto an Inca substrate.

So the diachronic problem isn't just "did the system drift?" It's that **the bilingual evidence comes from a different functional register than the monolingual evidence you're trying to decode.** The khipu boards are to imperial khipus what a modern spreadsheet with a sticky note attached is to a medieval abacus — same medium, different epistemic context.

## 3. The dialect continuum — now testable

This is where the new computational work is most directly relevant. Urton's seven-variable model (fiber choice, color spectrum, S/Z plying, recto/verso attachment, S/Z knotting, number class, decimal/non-decimal type) is, as you note, essentially a **feature list for a dialect continuum** — and for years it's been treated as a set of "parameters" to be standardized rather than as *signal*.

The **Khipu Computational Analysis Toolkit (K-CAT)**, published in 2026, is the first infrastructure that makes the dialect-continuum hypothesis testable at scale. It's built on the Khipu Field Guide dataset (709 khipus, ~3–4 person-years of expert annotation) and includes a **provenance breakdown that maps which arithmetic patterns cluster at which Andean sites**. That's the first computational test of whether the "noise" in the corpus is actually regional variation carrying meaning.

The **Medrano & Khosla (2024)** analysis of 650 khipus found that Ascher formulae (internal sum relationships) characterize **74% of the corpus**, but that top cords register a minority of sum relationships and function as markers of "working" khipus. The 26% that don't follow Ascher patterns is where the dialectal/functional variation likely lives — and it's where the categorical variables are most likely to be doing non-numerical work.

## The interface question — what's actually testable now

Your reframing is correct: the question isn't "what word does this knot say?" but **"how does the categorical layer constrain the numerical data, and vice versa?"** Three specific tests are now possible:

**Test 1: Kayte-to-data constraint.** Hyland's 2020 paper on "Subject Indicators" (kaytes) is the key finding here. The *kayte* — the colorful bundle of pendants at the *beginning* of a khipu, before any knotted data — functions as a **genre/topic marker**. A red deer-hair kayte means "this khipu is about warfare on behalf of the Inca king." This is a direct interface: the categorical layer (color, fiber at position 1) constrains the *interpretive frame* for the numerical data that follows. If you can computationally correlate kayte features with the *type* of arithmetic structure that follows (e.g., tribute sums vs. census counts vs. labor tallies), you've identified a categorical-to-numerical mapping without needing a bilingual text.

**Test 2: Color/fiber as sum-relationship selectors.** The 74% of khipus that follow Ascher patterns and the 26% that don't — do the categorical variables predict which set a khipu falls into? If, say, khipus with predominantly red pendants consistently show one type of sum structure and khipus with predominantly white pendants show another, you've identified a categorical variable that *selects the arithmetic operation*. That's not a Rosetta Stone — it's an internal structural constraint that's testable across the full 709-khipu corpus.

**Test 3: Benford's Law as a null model.** A 2024 computational investigation at Harvard used Benford's Law to test whether khipu knot values look like "real" numerical data (e.g., population counts, tribute amounts) or something else. If the knot values *don't* follow Benford's distribution in the same way that, say, census data does, that's evidence that some of the "numbers" are actually **non-numerical sign values** using the knot system as a notational convenience. This is a way to identify which subset of the numerical layer is actually categorical data in disguise.

## What this means for the Piedra del Sol analogy

The khipu is *not* in the Piedra del Sol situation, and the interface question is the proof. The Piedra del Sol has no internal structure to test — it's a single static surface with no co-occurring subsystem. The khipu has **two co-occurring subsystems on the same artifact**, one of which is solved. That means the deep structure is *constrained by the surface form in a way the Sun Stone isn't*. You don't need an external decoder; you need to find the **mapping function between the two layers**, and that mapping function is testable computationally against 700+ artifacts without a single bilingual text.

The khipu is closer to the **Dresden Codex** than to the Piedra del Sol: the form and the rules co-survived, but in *separate subsystems* rather than in a single unified text. The task isn't "find the Rosetta Stone" — it's "find the interface," and the interface is where the categorical and numerical layers meet. That's exactly where the new computational work is pointing.

## Chachapoyas (northern Peru) — the strongest signal

K-CAT Phase 4 reports **Chachapoyas at 52% Complex** (multiple simultaneous summation types) versus **Central Coast at 8%** — the largest geographic contrast in the corpus. The Chachapoya khipus (KH0242, KH0245, KH0257 from Leymebamba, 874 / 164 / 162 cords respectively) are the ones where Urton first identified **three-term numerical sequences** he interpreted as possible "identity labels" — a non-Ascher function where the knot values aren't doing arithmetic but serving as signifiers. They also carry the highest density of **non-decimal knots** (knots below the unit register, or additional knots after the unit position) in the corpus. This is the clearest case of a site with a *different arithmetic logic* rather than just a different application of the same logic.

## Inkawasi (Cañete Valley, central coast) — a distinct operation

The Inkawasi khipu archive (Clindaniel & Chu 2016, Sector B) shows a pattern that is *not* standard Ascher summation: **repeating constant values in three-cord sequences of the form *y* − *n* = *x***, where *n* is fixed (15, 47, or 208 depending on the khipu). Paired khipus (UR267A/UR267B vs. UR255/UR256) record the *same data* but in **inverted form** — one set uses *x* / *n* / *y*, the other *x* / *y* / *n*. This is a **partition-and-subtract** operation, not an additive hierarchy. It's a different arithmetic *grammar* operating on the same medium. The "Sector B" khipus with larger values may be archival registers, distinct from the working accounts.

## Puruchuco (Rímac River, near Lima) — hierarchical aggregation

The Puruchuco archive (21 khipus, excavated 1956) shows a **three-level vertical summation hierarchy** (Brezine & Villacorta 2020): Level I local accounts → Level II village-level sums → Level III regional synthesis. The top-level khipus carry **introductory segments of three figure-eight knots** that Urton and Brezine interpret as a site identifier (the palace of Puruchuco). This is Ascher-adjacent but organized as a **bureaucratic aggregation protocol** rather than an internal cord-level relationship. The checks-and-balances mechanism (identical or matching values across horizontal levels) is a functional property not seen at other sites.

## The 26% non-Ascher subset — where it lives

Medrano & Khosla (2024) found that **74% of the 650-khipu corpus** follows Ascher formulae. The remaining 26% is where the site-specific variation concentrates. The K-CAT Phase 4 analysis uses **chi-square tests and nearest-neighbor attribution** to map which of the nine summation pattern types cluster at which zones. The key finding: **PP (pattern prevalence) and IS (internal sum) rates are statistically significant by zone**, meaning the arithmetic behavior is not uniform across the Andean landscape.

However, the K-CAT FAQ is explicit about a limitation: **provenance semantics for color are NOT supported (p = 1.00)**. The geographic signal is in the *structural and arithmetic* features, not in the color coding. The provenance metadata quality also varies — many khipus in museum collections have only coarse provenance (a valley, a region), which limits the spatial resolution of the clustering.

## KH0082/KH0083 — the "Large" and "Layered" khipus

These two (Museo Chileno de Arte Precolombino, provenance uncertain but likely central Andean) are the single most detailed case study of non-Ascher features. KH0082 has **38 of 394 knotted cords with non-decimal knots**; KH0083 has **23 of 358**. The anomalous **eighth cluster** of KH0083 — spaced beyond the main bunching, with non-decimal knots on pendants p85–p88 and their subsidiaries — is flagged as a possible site of **non-numeric encoding**. If that's correct, it's a **local or individual convention** — a micro-dialect within the knot system.

## What the assumption actually says

You're positing a three-layer structure:

1. **Deep structure** — the semantically compressed preservation artifact: the living administrative, social, and cosmological system of the Inca state. Not a text, not an object — a *distributed cognitive architecture* embedded in social organization, landscape, ritual, labor, and oral tradition.
2. **Generative grammar** — the rules that project from that deep structure into specific surface forms. The *administrative logic*: what data gets recorded, how it's aggregated, what the categorical variables select, which arithmetic operation applies to which context.
3. **Surface form** — the khipu itself: a *snapshot* of the grammar applied to a specific problem domain at a specific time. A tribute computation for a specific ayllu in a specific year. A census of laborers in a specific district. A record of a specific administrative act.

The khipu is not the *source* of the information. It's a **derived output** — a printout from a computation whose interpreter (the cultural system) is gone.

## Why this changes the decoding problem

The standard framing — "crack the code, find the Rosetta Stone" — treats the khipu as a *self-contained encoding* whose rules are internal to the artifact. Your framing says the rules are **external to the artifact** — they're in the culture that produced it. The khipu is a *trace of a computation*, not the computation itself.

This has five specific implications:

### 1. The "dialect variation" is input variation, not grammatical variation

The Chachapoya "different arithmetic logic" (52% complex vs. 8% on the central coast) is not a different grammar. It's the **same grammar applied to a different problem domain**. The Chachapoya administrative system had different relationships — different tribute structure, different social organization, possibly a pre-Inca substrate — so the *input* was different, and therefore the *output* (the arithmetic patterns) is different.

The "dialect continuum" you hypothesized earlier is actually a **function space**: the same rules, different inputs. The variation across sites is not noise to be standardized away — it's *signal about the problem domain*. The K-CAT Phase 4 geographic clustering is not mapping dialects. It's mapping **administrative contexts**.

### 2. The non-Ascher 26% is a different *function*, not a different *grammar*

The Ascher patterns (74%) are one class of computation — additive hierarchy, like a `SUM` or `AGGREGATE` function. The non-Ascher patterns (26%) are a different class:

- **Inkawasi** (*y* − *n* = *x*): a `DIFFERENCE` or `PARTITION` function
- **Chachapoya** three-term sequences: a `LOOKUP` or `IDENTITY` function
- **Puruchuco** three-level vertical hierarchy: a `REDUCE` or `FOLD` function with a specific aggregation protocol

They're all the same grammar, just different *operations*. The categorical layer (color, fiber, twist) is the **function selector** — it specifies *which operation* to apply. The numerical layer is the *data being operated on*. The interface between the two layers is the **function call**.

This means the "interface question" you identified earlier is not a mapping to be discovered. It's a **function signature** to be reconstructed: *given these categorical features, which arithmetic operation is being applied, and to what data?*

### 3. The decoding problem is an inverse problem, not a translation problem

You're not trying to "read" the khipu. You're trying to **reconstruct the input** (the problem domain, the social context, the administrative situation) from the **output** (the khipu). This is like trying to reconstruct the database query from the report printout.

The "Rosetta Stone" isn't a bilingual text. It's a **known input-output pair** — a khipu whose *administrative context* is independently documented. The Medrano & Urton (2018) Santa Valley match (132 tribute payers in the colonial document ↔ 132 color-coded cord groups on the khipu) is the closest thing to this, but it only confirms the *numerical* layer. The categorical layer — the function selector — is still unconstrained.

### 4. The "ubiquitous expression" means the decoder is distributed and lost

Your phrase "ubiquitous expression in the culture" is the key. The deep structure isn't just in the khipu — it's *everywhere* in the culture:

- **Social organization**: the ayllu, curaca, khipukamayuq hierarchy
- **Landscape**: administrative boundaries, storage facilities (*qollqa*), road networks
- **Ritual**: the New Fire Ceremony, the calendar
- **Labor system**: mit'a, tribute, redistribution
- **Oral tradition**: the narratives that the khipu "encoded"

All of these were destroyed or transformed by the conquest. The khipu is a *fossil of a computation* whose *interpreter* is gone. The redundancy that would have allowed cross-checking (the khipu against the landscape, against the ritual, against the oral tradition) is lost. You're left with a single expression that can't be validated against the others.

This is the exact same failure mode as the Piedra del Sol — but with a crucial difference: the khipu has **internal structure** (the two co-occurring subsystems, one solved) that constrains the inverse problem. The Piedra del Sol has no such internal constraint. The khipu is *partially self-decoding* in a way the Sun Stone isn't.

### 5. The Piedra del Sol is the deep structure; the khipu is a projection from it

This inverts the analogy we've been using. The Piedra del Sol *is* the semantically compressed preservation artifact — the deep structure made visible in stone. The khipu is a *projection from* that kind of deep structure. The khipu is one level removed from the source.

So the relationship is:

| Layer | Piedra del Sol | Khipu |
|---|---|---|
| Deep structure | The stone itself (cosmological encoding) | The Inca administrative system (distributed in culture) |
| Generative grammar | Lost (no rules for reading the stone) | Partially recoverable (Ascher patterns reveal the *type* of computation) |
| Surface form | The concentric rings, day-signs | The knots, colors, fibers, structure |
| Status | **Fossil** — form without rules | **Computation trace** — form with partial rules |

The Piedra del Sol is a *snapshot of the deep structure itself*. The khipu is a *snapshot of a computation performed on the deep structure*. The khipu is one level further from the source, but it has the advantage of internal structure that constrains the inverse problem.

## What's testable under this framing

The inverse problem is tractable in principle, and the testable predictions are:

**Prediction 1: The categorical layer should predict the arithmetic function.**
If color/fiber/twist are function selectors, then khipus with the same categorical profile should show the same arithmetic pattern, *regardless of site*. This is a direct test of the "function signature" hypothesis. The K-CAT toolkit can test this across the 709-khipu corpus.

**Prediction 2: The numerical layer should be invertible given the function.**
If you know the function (e.g., additive hierarchy) and the output (the knot values), you can invert the computation to recover the *input* (the individual tribute amounts, the population counts, the labor assignments). The inverted values should be *plausible* — they should match the scale of known Inca administrative data (e.g., ayllu populations of a few hundred, tribute amounts in the range documented by colonial sources).

**Prediction 3: The "non-Ascher" 26% should cluster by *function type*, not by site.**
If the non-Ascher patterns are different *functions* (partition, identity, reduce) rather than different *grammars*, they should cluster by the *type of administrative task* they encode, not by geographic origin. A partition function should appear at *any* site where the administrative task requires partitioning, regardless of whether it's Chachapoyas or the central coast.

**Prediction 4: The kayte (introductory bundle) should be the function header.**
Hyland's finding that the kayte is a "subject indicator" fits this framing exactly: it's the **function call header** — it specifies *what kind of computation* follows. A red deer-hair kayte = "this is a warfare computation." A white cotton kayte = "this is a tribute computation." The kayte is the *function name*; the rest of the khipu is the *function body*.

## The honest limitation

The inverse problem is only as good as the *function library* you can reconstruct. You can invert a computation if you know the function. The Ascher patterns give you *one* function (additive hierarchy). The Inkawasi patterns give you *another* (partition-subtract). But the full function library — the complete set of administrative operations the Inca system performed — is not recoverable from the khipus alone. It's recoverable only from the *culture*, which is gone.

This is the fundamental limit of your framing: the khipu is a *trace of a computation*, and you can only invert a computation if you know the function. The function is in the culture, not in the artifact. The artifact tells you *that* a computation was performed and *what the output was*. It does not tell you *what the function was*, except in the cases where the internal structure (the Ascher patterns, the categorical correlations) constrains it.

The Piedra del Sol has the same limit, but worse: it has no internal structure to constrain the inverse problem at all. The khipu is *closer* to the source than the Sun Stone, because the computation trace contains *partial information about the function*. But it's still one level removed, and the full function library is in the culture that was destroyed.

The khipu is not a code to be cracked. It's a **computation trace to be inverted**, and the inversion is only possible to the extent that the function library can be reconstructed from the internal structure of the traces themselves. That's what the K-CAT work is doing, and it's the right approach — but the ceiling is set by what the traces can tell you about the function, and that ceiling is low.

## The re-traversal is already happening

They've identified a **hierarchical two-scale structure**: 26 infinite bars corresponding to macro-continental circuits (the primary suyus routes) and 6 finite bars corresponding to meso-regional circuits. This is the topology of the administrative system, reconstructed from the physical infrastructure.

The Brewminate synthesis puts it in the exact terms of your framing: *"Roads, runners, and khipus formed a single administrative ecology. The road supplied movement; the chasqui supplied speed; the khipu supplied memory; the state supplied the demand that made all three necessary."* The khipu was not a record *about* the topology. It was **the memory layer of the topology itself**.

The traversable substrate has four layers, and all four are partially intact:

The capillary network is gone, but the macro-structure is reconstructable. The persistent homology paper has done the macro-structure.

## What the re-traversal provides for khipu interpretation

Your claim is that re-establishing the topology of cognition provides the **interpretive basis** for the khipu. Concretely, this means:

**The categorical layer becomes a topological coordinate.** If you know the node structure (which tambó, which qollqa, which curaca residence), the edge structure (which road segment, which direction of flow), and the hierarchy (which level of aggregation: ayllu → ayllullu → waranqi → tocopa → waranqa → hunu → chamapa → waranqa → suyu), then the color/fiber/twist variables on a khipu are **addresses in that topology**, not abstract "function selectors." A khipu from a specific qollqa at a specific node in a specific suyu has a **topological identity** that constrains what its categorical variables can mean.

**The numerical layer becomes a state vector at that coordinate.** The knot values are the contents of the qollqa, the labor assignment for that ayllu, the tribute obligation for that waranqi. The arithmetic pattern (Ascher additive, Inkawasi partition, Chachapoya identity) is the **aggregation operation appropriate to that node's position in the hierarchy**. A leaf node (ayllu) does local accounting. A branch node (curaca center) does aggregation. A trunk node (suyu capital) does synthesis. The arithmetic pattern is a function of topological position.

The variation is in the topology, not in the grammar.

## The critical limitation

The topology is **fragmented**. The 2026 cartographic analysis is explicit: "The system no longer retains its capillary territorial model, which once articulated settlements with a distant imperial center." You can reconstruct the macro-structure (the 26 continental circuits, the 6 meso-regional circuits, the four suyus). You can reconstruct the major nodes (Cusco, Huánuco Pampa, Vilcashuamán, Xauxa, Cajamarca). But the **capillary network** — the local ayllu-level connections, the specific qollqa assignments, the individual curaca jurisdictions — is lost.

This means the re-traversal provides a **coarse-grained interpretive framework**, not a fine-grained one. You can say "this khipu is from a trunk node in Collasuyu, so it should show a three-level aggregation hierarchy." You cannot say "this khipu is from the qollqa at tambó 47 on the road between Xauxa and Tarmatambo, so the red pendants correspond to the specific tribute category for that segment." The capillary resolution is gone.

But the coarse-grained framework is still a **massive constraint** that no amount of purely internal khipu analysis can provide. The K-CAT Phase 4 geographic clustering (Chachapoya 52% complex vs. Central Coast 8%) is exactly the kind of signal that the topological reconstruction explains: the Chachapoya region was a **different part of the topology** with a different administrative structure, so the cross-sections look different. The re-traversal doesn't decode the khipu. It **constrains the space of possible decodings** from infinite to finite, and that's what makes the problem tractable.

## The Tupicocha case as proof of principle

Not a Rosetta Stone — a **living traversal** that constrains the interpretation of the frozen cross-sections.

That's what your claim amounts to, and it's the right one. The khipu is not a code to be cracked. The Piedra del Sol is explicitly rooted in older Mesoamerican traditions. The stone's iconography draws on several layers of prior cultural heritage:

**Calendrical tradition:** The 20 day-signs of the tonalpohualli (260-day ritual calendar) are a pan-Mesoamerican system with roots going back at least to the Maya and Olmec periods — well over a millennium before the stone was carved.

**Five Suns cosmology:** Multiple pre-Mexica Nahua-speaking peoples used variants of the five-era creation structure. The specific sequence (Four Jaguar → Four Wind → Four Rain → Four Water → Four Movement) was a specifically Aztec configuration, but the underlying framework of successive world ages destroyed by cataclysms is shared across Mesoamerican cultures.

**Olmec and early Mesoamerican precedent:** Pre-Aztec cosmological stone carving appears at sites like Chalcatzingo (ca. 700–500 BCE), and the concentric-ring, solar-disk format has antecedents in earlier Mesoamerican monumental sculpture.

**Toltec layer:** The stone's function as a _cuauhxicalli_ (eagle vessel / sacrificial platform) and its connection to the New Fire Ceremony tie it to Toltec ritual practice. Scholar Khristaan Villela has argued the stone functioned as a "Turquoise Hearth" — a sacred bundle channeling cosmological power — a concept with deep Toltec and Chichimec origins.

So while the _specific composition_ of the Piedra del Sol is a product of Mexica political authority under Moctezuma II, every major element it encodes — the calendar, the creation myth, the ritual function, the iconographic vocabulary — is drawn from traditions stretching back roughly **2,000 years** through the Toltec, Maya, and Olmec civilizations.
