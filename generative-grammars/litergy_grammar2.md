This is a 2023 article by **Miklós István Földváry** in the _Church Life Journal_ (Notre Dame's McGrath Institute), describing the **Usuarium** project — a digital library and database for the study of Latin liturgical history in the Middle Ages and Early Modern Period.

**Core idea:** Usuarium is a _map-view of liturgical diversity_. It organizes service books (missals, pontificals, ordinals, etc.) by the ecclesiastical institution and geographic location that produced them, treating each institution's distinct liturgical tradition as a **use**.

**Key methodological principles:**

- **Mapping over chronology** — places are fixed, so the geographic grid is the stable coordinate for comparing uses across centuries.
- **Precedence of printed sources** — early printed service books are treated as highly reliable snapshots of late-medieval uses, rather than dismissed as "post-medieval." This enables a reverse-chronological approach: establish the late-15th/early-16th-century baseline first, then trace distinctive features backward.
- **Standardized coding** — uniform tags for book types, rites, and liturgical positions (assignments) allow systematic comparison of variation across 1,500+ sources.

**Scale:** By the time of writing, the project had indexed over 1,000 sources, with the "Missalia Project" covering full missals of more than 180 European institutions and over a million records.

**Origin:** The project grew out of Földváry's doctoral work on Hungarian ordinals and pontificals, where he discovered that eleventh-century Hungarian pontificals and fifteenth-century ordinals shared a continuous "use" of Esztergom — while twelfth- and thirteenth-century books belonged to _different_ Hungarian traditions. This led him to develop the comparative, map-based method now embodied in Usuarium.

It's a distinct topic from the earlier "generative liturgy" discussion: here the focus is on **digital humanities infrastructure for comparative liturgical scholarship**, not on liturgy as a generative process for community formation.

---

The Usuarium project bears directly on the orthodoxy/orthopraxy question in a revealing way:

**The core tension it exposes:** The medieval Latin West shared a high degree of **orthodoxy** (creedal, doctrinal unity) while exhibiting extraordinary diversity in **orthopraxy** (liturgical practice). Usuarium's data — 180+ diocesan uses, each treated as "equal traditions in their own right" — demonstrates that correct belief did not entail a single correct practice. A priest in Mainz and one in Esztergom could be doctrinally identical yet perform markedly different rites.

**This contrasts sharply with the Eastern tradition.** In Byzantine theology, _praxis_ (correct worship) is treated as essentially **conjoint with orthodoxy** — "theology without action is the theology of demons" (Maximus the Confessor). Orthopraxis is "right glory" or "right worship," and is understood as the very expression of correct faith, not merely its outward sign. The Eastern Churches maintained a far tighter coupling between the two.

**Usuarium's methodological stance is itself an argument about orthopraxy.** By treating each local use as a legitimate tradition rather than a deviation from a norm, Földváry implicitly rejects the idea that there was one "correct" Latin liturgical practice before the Council of Trent. The project's framing — "variation, not evolution" — means no single use is the _telos_ toward which others deviate. This is a historically grounded challenge to the post-Tridentine assumption (reinforced by the Roman Missal of 1570 and its subsequent imposition) that a single orthopraxy was always the norm.

**The broader implication:** Usuarium's data suggests that in the Latin West, orthodoxy and orthopraxy were **decoupled** in a way they were not in the East. Doctrinal unity coexisted with liturgical pluralism for centuries. The Tridentine and post-Tridentine standardization — which Usuarium documents as the "final abolition of local variants in the late 17th century" — was the moment when a single orthopraxy was finally imposed across the Latin West, bringing it closer to the Eastern model of liturgical uniformity as an expression of doctrinal unity.

In short: Usuarium provides the empirical evidence that medieval Latin Christianity was far more **orthodox than orthopraxically uniform** than the post-Reformation narrative typically assumes.

This raises the question was the diversity in orthopraxy a form of orthogonal actions leading to greater opportunities of re-entry to equally valid expressions that together formed a more robust preservation of the orthodoxy than one could alone, or was it just deviation over time?

---

This is a genuinely open question in liturgical scholarship, but the evidence from Usuarium and related work tilts decisively toward the **orthogonal/robustness** reading over the drift reading — with an important structural caveat.

**Against drift, for deliberate differentiation:**

Földváry's own data is the key finding: _"the amount of divergence is greater than we would expect from the spontaneous variation of available ritual elements. Variation does not come about by itself as a result of natural, dialect-like evolution. Instead, it was someone at some point in history who deliberately worked on differentiation."_ The diversity is **artificial** — engineered, maintained, and defended — not accumulated noise. A use's distinctive features persist for centuries as stable, identifiable "fingerprints," not as a random walk away from an origin.

**The interpretive framework as the binding mechanism:**

The Cambridge study on medieval _rationes_ (Amalar of Metz, Durandus) shows that the orthodoxy was preserved not by uniform practice but by an **allegorical reasoning apparatus** specifically designed to harmonize diversity: _"the analogical ratio provides an explanatory framework that is rich enough in potential elements to accommodate different practices and lend a harmonious unity to them all."_ This is the meta-level that makes the orthogonal model coherent: the shared doctrinal core is _expressed_ through different local configurations, and the interpretive tradition treats all of them as valid instantiations of the same truth. The orthodoxy is in the _structure of the framework_, not in any single node.

**Active complementarity, not passive drift:**

The St Andrews work on early-medieval sacramentaries documents that _"monastic and religious centres actively sought out complementary local liturgy that supplemented the more generally used Carolingian material with that from different origins."_ Communities were _deliberately_ gathering diverse liturgical material. This is the robustness mechanism in action: each use is an independent preservation channel, and the system as a whole preserves more than any single channel could.

**The structural caveat — it's not fully orthogonal:**

The shared Roman heritage forms a very large common core. The "orthogonal" elements are the _peripheral_ features: local saints' feasts, local prayers, local ceremonial details, the euchological layer that Usuarium's Mass analysis shows varies significantly between uses. So the model is better described as **hub-and-spoke with a very large hub**: a shared structural skeleton (the Roman rite's architecture) with genuinely independent local features radiating from it. The orthogonality is real but partial — it's in the _local_ layer, not the _universal_ layer.

**The robustness argument is strongest at the periphery:**

Local elements are the most vulnerable to loss (a community disappears, its unique prayers vanish). Multiple uses mean multiple independent copies of the broader tradition, each preserving different subsets. The Tridentine abolition of local variants — which Usuarium documents as completing by the late 17th century — was, from this perspective, a **reduction in redundancy**: the system went from many independent preservation channels to one, making it more fragile, not more faithful.

**In short:** the evidence supports the reading that medieval liturgical diversity was a _deliberate, interpretively-harmonized, conservatively-maintained_ system of partially orthogonal expressions whose collective coverage of the tradition was more robust than any single use could achieve. It was not drift. But the orthogonality is bounded by the shared Roman core — it's a system of _structured variation_, not of independent axes.

---

The Tridentine reform (Council of Trent, 1545–63; implemented in the 1570 Missal of Pius V) reduced redundancy by **pruning late-medieval accretions** from the liturgical books. Specifically:

- **Sanctoral cycle cut back** – The very full calendar of saints' feasts was substantially reduced to restore the precedence of the temporal (seasonal) cycle, especially in Lent.
- **Votive Masses curtailed** – Their number was reduced and their use strictly restricted to weekday ferias.
- **Sequences purged** – The poetic sequences sung before the Gospel were removed, with only a handful retained (Easter, Pentecost, Corpus Christi, Requiem).
- **Books standardized and reorganized** – Existing liturgical books were pruned, reorganized, and reissued under papal authority, eliminating "rank and monstrous excesses" that had crept in during the later Middle Ages. More recent accretions of doubtful historical value were dropped outright.

The overall effect was a single, uniform Roman Missal that could be printed identically everywhere, replacing the patchwork of local diocesan variants with their overlapping and duplicated material.

---

The late-medieval accretions weren't driven by a single motive but by several reinforcing forces:

**Relic translations.** The single "really determining factor" in the early growth was the transfer of a martyr's relics to a new community, which almost always brought with it the institution of an annual feast. As relics spread geographically, so did the feasts attached to them.

**New religious orders.** After the 12th century, the calendar became "a reflection of the Church's life," and the newly founded orders (Franciscans, Dominicans, and later Jesuits) pushed for their own members to be inscribed. The driving assumption was that in a world seen as increasingly un-Christian, sanctity flourished only in the cloisters — so the orders had a claim on liturgical space.

**New devotional interests.** The Catholic Encyclopedia names this "the potent factor" behind feasts for All Souls, the Trinity, the Angels, Corpus Christi, the Sacred Heart, the Five Wounds, and numerous Marian invocations. These arose from popular piety, confraternities, and a growing desire to sacralize more of the year.

**The desire to fill every day.** The martyrologia compilers (Florus, Ado, Usuard) worked with the "avowed object of filling up the days" left blank by their predecessors — a kind of liturgical _horror vacui_.

**Centralization of authority.** As the power to approve new feasts shifted from local bishops to Rome, the calendar became more uniform but also more crowded, since Rome was the sole gatekeeper for what could be added.

In short, the accretions were the cumulative product of **popular devotion, institutional competition among orders, relic commerce, and a theological conviction that more saints on the calendar meant more intercessors for the faithful** — not a deliberate program of bloat, but an organic, unregulated growth that no one was trimming until Trent.

---

Each feast was a full liturgical _package_ — not just a name in a column. It carried:

- A **proper collect** articulating a specific theological emphasis (e.g., this saint as intercessor for a particular need, or as a witness to a particular mystery)
- **Proper readings** (Epistle and Gospel) chosen to frame the saint within a particular aspect of salvation history
- **Proper antiphons and responsories** for the Divine Office, often drawing on the saint's hagiography, attributes, and symbolic associations
- A **rank** (double, semi-double, simple) that determined how much of the local celebration it displaced
- A **patronage** and a set of **attributes** (the martyr's palm, the virgin's lily, the scholar's book) that gave the feast its iconographic and mnemonic identity

So the "redundancy" Trent was cutting wasn't just _quantitative_ clutter — it was **qualitative overlap**. You could have three separate days each celebrating a different facet of the Virgin, or four saints who were all "intercessors for the sick" with slightly different collect formulations, or a sequence for one martyr thematically colliding with the Gospel assigned to another. The semantic fields were _crowded and intersecting_, not merely numerous.

That's why the reform's logic wasn't just "delete feasts" but **re-assert the primacy of the temporal cycle** — the Easter cycle, the Incarnation cycle, the Paschal mystery — as the organizing semantic frame. The sanctoral feasts were to be _read through_ that frame, not to form a parallel, self-sufficient narrative of their own. The accretions had, over centuries, built up something close to a second liturgical narrative that competed with the first for the year's attention.

---

Why would they choose to prune rather than increase the semantic depth of the temporal cycle either by addition or merging?

---

Several reinforcing reasons, each pointing the same direction:

**The restoration mandate precludes synthesis.** Pius V's _Quo Primum_ frames the act as restoring the missal "to the original norm and rite of the holy fathers" (_ad pristinam…sanctorum patrum normam ac ritum_). You restore by _removing_ what was added later; you cannot restore by _creating_ a new hybrid that has no prior model. Merging the sanctoral into the temporal would have been an act of invention, not recovery — and invention was precisely what the reform was rejecting.

**The temporal cycle was already semantically saturated in its own mode.** Its depth is _sacramental-narrative_: it traces the arc of the Paschal mystery (Incarnation → Passion → Resurrection → Parousia) through a fixed sequence of seasons. The problem was never that this cycle was _thin_ — it was that it was being _interrupted and fragmented_ by the sanctoral. The remedy for fragmentation is to remove the fragments, not to absorb them.

**Different modes of liturgical action resist merging.** The temporal cycle operates as _mysterion_ — the sacramental re-presentation of Christ's own acts. The sanctoral operates as _commemoratio_ — the memorial of a saint's witness to those acts. These are categorically different. Merging a saint's intercessory collect into a Paschal-day formulary would conflate "the Church celebrates Christ's Resurrection" with "the Church remembers St. X's faith in that Resurrection" — a category error in the theology of liturgical time.

**The 1570 Missal actively proscribes the impulse to embellish.** It explicitly forbids the use of tropes — the very medieval practice of adding texts to augment existing chants. The reformers saw the drive to "add semantic depth" as the _disease_, not the cure. The accretions _were_ an attempt to deepen the liturgy, and the reform's verdict was that this had produced confusion, not richness.

**Uniformity and enforceability.** A pruned, fixed calendar could be printed identically in every diocese. A merged, enriched calendar would inevitably generate local variants again — different communities would merge different saints in different ways, recreating the patchwork the reform was eliminating. The 200-year rule in _Quo Primum_ (only rites older than two centuries were exempt) was designed to make any future "enrichment" require explicit papal authorization, not local initiative.

In short: the reformers' diagnosis was that the temporal cycle's semantic field was being _diluted_, not _starved_. The answer to dilution is to remove the diluting agent, not to thicken the thing being diluted.

If we look at it rather as an ecological state space consisting of attractors, sources, sinks, and the gradients established between them an adaptation of relative weight via addition to or merger of semantic fields could maintain the information and reinforce the structure.

---

The framing is apt, and it exposes a specific structural error in the Tridentine intervention.

**The system as state space.** The pre-Tridentine calendar was a landscape with:

- **Attractors**: the major temporal feasts (Easter, Christmas, Pentecost) — high-energy states that organized the year's flow
- **Sources**: the generative mechanisms (canonizations, relic translations, new orders) that continuously injected new nodes
- **Sinks**: the simple feasts where semantic content accumulated without being re-absorbed into the temporal gradient — information pooled at the low end of the rank hierarchy
- **Gradients**: the discrete rank system (double → semi-double → simple) that established the energy differential between attractors and the surrounding landscape

The pathology was that the sources were outpacing the sinks' capacity to dissipate, and the gradient was _flattening_ — too many nodes at similar weights, so the attractors lost their pull. The landscape was becoming a plain.

**Your proposal: reshape the energy surface, not the population.** Instead of removing nodes (pruning), you adjust the _relative weights_ on the existing topology — merge overlapping semantic fields into a single node with a richer formulary, or add new content to an existing node to deepen it, or reassign rank to steepen the gradient. The information is preserved; the structure is reinforced by making the attractors _steeper_ relative to their neighbors rather than by _removing_ the neighbors.

This is ecologically more stable. Removing a species doesn't just remove its node — it removes the **edges** it maintained. The simple feasts that were pruned were the _low-energy texture_ of the landscape; they provided the contrast that made the attractors prominent. Remove them and you flatten the topology. The post-Tridentine calendar with its 157 ferial days was _topographically flatter_ than the pre-Tridentine one: the major feasts were more prominent, but the relief between them was reduced. The "hills" remained but the "valleys" had been filled.

**Why Trent didn't do this:**

1. **The restorative paradigm has no merge operation.** You restore by removing what was added. You cannot "merge your way back" to a lost original — the merged configuration has no precedent to cite. The act is legible as _correction_ (we removed what was added) or as _innovation_ (we created something new). In the Counter-Reformation, that distinction was the entire political point.

2. **The rank system was discrete, not continuous.** The existing weight-adaptation mechanism (double, semi-double, simple) had only a few levels. You could demote a feast, but you couldn't say "this node has 0.7 the weight of that one." There was no mechanism for _continuous_ weight adjustment — only _categorical_ reassignment. Your proposal requires a finer-grained gradient than the system's ontology allowed.

3. **Merging is a local operation; the goal was a universal book.** "Merge these three collects into one" depends on which three are present in a given diocese. You can't print a single Missal that prescribes a merge when the merge-sets differ by locale. Pruning is _universal_ — remove node X everywhere. Merging is _contextual_ — it recreates the patchwork the reform was eliminating.

**Where your proposal actually materialized — belatedly.** Vatican II's _Mysterii Paschalis_ (1969) did something structurally close: it kept more saints in the calendar than Trent had, but introduced a **finer-grained gradient** (solemnity → feast → memorial → _optional memorial_) and repositioned the temporal cycle's attractors by _structural priority_ rather than by node removal. The "optional memorial" is precisely a new low-energy state — a way to occupy a node locally without disrupting the universal gradient. That's weight adaptation, not pruning.

The cost of the Tridentine pruning, in your terms, is that it **reduced the dimensionality of the state space** when the actual problem was a **degradation of the vector field**. The attractors weren't weak; the gradient between them and the surrounding nodes had been eroded by uniform accumulation. The correct intervention was to _steepen the gradient_ (re-rank, merge, deepen existing nodes), not to _delete the nodes that constituted the gradient's lower bound_.

"recreates the patchwork" if the application of local variation was understood as part of a recursive applications of established patterns and the expression of this diversity as a method of reinforcement of preservation of sufficiency for maintenance and reconstruction in response to internal and external pressures, components in the tapestry of the whole.

---

"Patchwork" was the Tridentine reform's _own_ framing, and it does work by collapsing a distinction that matters.

**Two categories of local variation, treated as one.**

Pre-Tridentine variation was not a single phenomenon. It was at least two:

1. **Recursive instantiation.** The great rites — Roman, Ambrosian, Mozarabic, Gallican, Sarum — each operated as a _self-consistent generative system_. A new feast added within the Sarum rite was not an arbitrary insertion; it was _instantiated through the rite's own grammar_: its collect followed the Sarum collect's syntactic and theological patterns, its readings were drawn from the Sarum lectionary's logic, its rank was assigned within the Sarum rank hierarchy. The local variation was _pattern-governed_ — a recursive application of established structure. This is the "tapestry" you're describing: each thread is locally distinct but woven from the same warp.

2. **Unstructured accretion.** A diocese adding a feast for a newly translated relic without a proper collect, or a confraternity pushing a votive Mass into a Sunday, or a bishop introducing a sequence with no liturgical precedent. This is _ad hoc_ — no governing pattern, no recursive structure, no internal consistency with the surrounding calendar.

The Tridentine reform's diagnosis was that the system was dominated by category 2. The intervention — uniformity — was calibrated to eliminate category 2. But it _also_ eliminated category 1, because the mechanism of enforcement (a single printed book, a two-century rule, papal authorization for any deviation) had no resolution fine enough to distinguish "this local variation is a recursive instantiation of a coherent rite" from "this local variation is an arbitrary accretion." Both were simply _deviation from the Roman norm_, and both were suppressed.

**The resilience argument.**

Your framing of local variation as "maintenance and reconstruction in response to internal and external pressures" is the standard complexity-theory resilience argument: a system with _structured_ local diversity has more attractor basins, more gradient paths, more capacity to absorb perturbation without global phase transition. A uniform system has a single attractor; perturb it enough and the whole thing shifts state at once.

The post-Tridentine calendar's vulnerability is visible in its history. The 17th- and 18th-century _consuetudines_ controversies (where dioceses fought to retain local customs that the 1570 Missal had technically abolished) were not a failure of enforcement — they were the system _demanding_ the local variation it had been denied, because the uniform structure was insufficient for the pressures it faced. The French Revolution's attack on the uniform Roman rite was a single external perturbation that hit a single attractor; there was no local redundancy to absorb it. The pre-Tridentine system, with its multiple rite-structures, would have had _multiple basins_ — the destruction of one did not collapse the others.

**What the recursive model would have looked like.**

If the reformers had understood the system as you describe — a generative grammar with local instantiation — the intervention would have been:

- **Codify the grammar, not the output.** Specify the _rules_ (collect structure, lectionary logic, rank hierarchy, seasonal priority) rather than the _instances_ (this specific text on this specific day). Each rite or diocese would instantiate the grammar locally.
- **Preserve the recursive structure of the great rites** as legitimate attractor basins, while eliminating only the _unstructured_ accretions that violated even their local grammar.
- **Maintain the gradient** by ensuring that the generative rules _steepened_ the hierarchy (temporal > sanctoral > votive) without _deleting_ the lower nodes.

This is, roughly, what the 1963 _Sacrosanctum Concilium_ attempted with its "limited vernacular" and "inculturation" provisions, and what the 1970 Missal's _Ordo Missae_ (fixed) vs. _Proprium de Tempore_ (variable) structure actually implements — a grammar with local instantiation slots. It's a partial recovery of the recursive model, applied to a system that had been uniform for four centuries.

**The cost of the Tridentine collapse.**

In your terms: the reform converted a _multi-basin landscape_ (multiple rites, each with its own attractor structure) into a _single-basin landscape_ (one Roman rite, one attractor). It eliminated the unstructured noise (category 2) — good. But it also eliminated the structured local diversity (category 1) — the actual _resilience mechanism_. The system became cleaner but more fragile. The "tapestry" was not a patchwork; it was a _lattice of interdependent local structures_ that collectively maintained the whole. Cutting it down to a single uniform thread didn't remove the noise — it removed the _redundancy that made the noise tolerable_.

Exactly. The pilgrimage gradient was a _liturgical_ gradient as much as a geographical one.

Pre-Tridentine, the pilgrim's journey was a **transition between attractor basins**. The home diocese operated under its local _usus_ — its own collect structures, its own lectionary logic, its own proper chants for the sanctoral. The destination — Rome, Santiago, Canterbury, the Holy Land — operated under a _different instantiation_ of the same recursive grammar. The pilgrim arrived in a semantically familiar but structurally distinct space: the same deep rules, a different surface. That was the "new recognizable experience." The travel was not just spatial; it was a **traversal of the state space itself**, and the liturgical novelty at the destination was part of what justified the cost and risk of the journey.

The Tridentine intervention **collapsed the gradient between home and destination**. When the Sarum pilgrim arrived in Rome and found the same Missal, the same collects, the same lectionary, the same rank hierarchy — the liturgical dimension of the travel was _zero_. The destination and the origin became the same attractor. The pilgrimage was reduced to a purely spatial translation within a single basin: you moved through physical distance but experienced no change in the liturgical field. The "new recognizable experience" was no longer there to be had.

This is visible in the historical record. The _Liturgy and Pilgrimage_ entry in the _Encyclopedia of Medieval Pilgrimage_ documents that liturgy was **integral** to the pilgrimage experience — the pilgrim's devotional act at the shrine was not just "being present at the relic" but _participating in that place's liturgical life_. The feast day of the patron saint, the proper collects, the local chant tradition — these were part of the _product_ the pilgrimage delivered. Uniformity removed that product.

What remained was the relic, the architecture, the indulgence. The **experiential differentiator** — the thing that made _this_ place liturgically _other_ than home — had been flattened. The pilgrimage became a visit to a museum of the same liturgy rather than a journey into a different expression of it.

And the irony is that the system _did_ resist the flattening. Le Brun's eighteenth-century _Liturgical Travels Through France_ documents how much "authentic liturgical diversity existed during a time of alleged Tridentine ossification" — the local variation kept re-emerging because the system _needed_ it. The pilgrimage economy, the devotional economy, the identity economy all demanded that the destination be _other_ than the origin. The uniform book couldn't suppress that demand; it could only make the suppression visible as a gap. The local _consuetudines_ that dioceses fought to retain in the 17th and 18th centuries were, in part, the system trying to **re-establish the pilgrimage gradient** — to make the destination _different_ again, because the experience of travel required it.

In your terms: the reform removed the **inter-basin gradient** and left only the **intra-basin uniformity**. The attractor was preserved, but the _landscape between attractors_ — which is where the pilgrimage actually happened — was flattened to zero.
